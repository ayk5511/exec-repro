\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Three literatures, three questions}\label{sec:three_literatures}

The word ``reproducibility'' names different problems in different fields, and
the distinction determines what our measurement can and cannot speak to.

The broadest strand asks whether published findings are \emph{true}.
\citet{Ioannidis2005} argued on statistical grounds that most published research
findings are false, and the \citet{OpenScienceCollaboration2015} supplied direct
evidence by rerunning a hundred psychology experiments with fresh data. In
empirical finance the corresponding work is \citet{HarveyLiuZhu2016}, who
recalibrate significance thresholds for a literature that has tested hundreds of
candidate return predictors, and \citet{HouXueZhang2020}, who rebuild the
anomaly literature under uniform methodology and find much of it does not
survive. These studies concern the durability of findings under new data or
stricter statistics. They are not about whether the original code runs.

A second strand asks whether an artifact is \emph{documented} well enough that
reproduction is possible in principle. \citet{GundersenKjensmo2018} score
artificial-intelligence papers on documentation dimensions and find most
underspecified; the \citet{PineauReproducibility2021} NeurIPS program turned
similar concerns into submission-time policy, introducing checklists and
artifact-availability expectations at a major venue. Instruments in this family,
including the disclosure rubric applied to machine-learning finance by
\citet{Khan2026Survey} and the audit-trail dimensions of
\citet{Khan2026MrAudit}, are scored by reading the paper and inspecting the
repository. They are cheap enough to apply at corpus scale, and they measure
disclosure.

The third question, and the one this paper takes up, is whether the released
artifact \emph{executes}. It sits between the other two: passing it is neither
necessary nor sufficient for a finding to be true, but failing it means no reader
can check the finding without reimplementing the work. The engineering literature
explains why this is hard even for competent authors.
\citet{SculleyTechnicalDebt2015} document how machine-learning systems accumulate
dependencies, configuration, and undeclared coupling faster than conventional
software, so a repository that ran on its author's machine drifts out of
executability without anyone touching it.

\subsection{Execution-based studies}\label{sec:exec_studies}

Several lines of work already run published code. Two are closest to ours in
method and in subject, and the present study is defined by how it differs from
each; the remainder matter because they bound what can honestly be claimed as new
here.

The first is at scale but outside finance. \citet{Trisovic2022} took more than
2,000 replication datasets from the Harvard Dataverse, comprising over 9,000 R
files, and executed them in a clean environment: 74\% of files failed on first
execution, falling to 56\% after automated code cleaning. That is the closest
methodological relative to what we do, and it establishes that large-scale
unassisted execution is both feasible and informative. It is also more
finely reported than a single rate: it defines ten research questions, separates
outcomes into a five-category error taxonomy, and breaks the re-execution rate
down by journal collection, by R version and year, and by research field.

Two things distinguish our setting. Its artifacts are replication packages
deposited in a curated archive, which a depositor prepared for that purpose,
rather than whatever address a paper offers its readers; and its error taxonomy
is organized by R-language error type, so while retrieval is separated and
quantified, environment declaration and environment construction are not scored
as distinct stages but handled as a code-cleaning step that installs missing
libraries. Those two stages are where most of our attrition occurs, which is why
we score them separately rather than repairing past them.

The second is in finance but under very different conditions.
\citet{Perignon2024} ran the code of 168 research teams answering six
prespecified questions, and found that running that code on the same raw data
regenerates exactly the same results only 52\% of the time. This is the
definitive finance result on computational reproducibility, and it is a
\emph{best-case} measurement by construction: the code was written by
participating teams for the purpose, the raw data were supplied, and the study
was coordinated. Those are precisely the conditions an ordinary reader of a
published paper does not have.

Two further studies bear directly on what is new here, and both predate us by
several years. \citet{Collberg2015}, published in shorter form as
\citet{CollbergProebsting2016}, took 601 papers from eight ACM conferences and
five journals, identified 402 whose results were backed by code, and attempted to
obtain and build it. They report an explicitly ordered three-level result, their
levels A, B and C: code was obtained and built within thirty minutes for 32.3\%
of papers (A), built with additional effort for 48.3\% (B), and either built by
them or asserted buildable by the authors for 54.0\% (C). The levels are nested,
so each rate is a prefix of the next. An ordered, cumulative scale for build
success is therefore not new, and we do not claim it. Their level A is moreover
framed explicitly as the condition in which communicating with the author is not
possible, which makes 32.3\% the closest prior figure to a cold-start
measurement. Two things still separate it from ours. Author email
was part of how code was \emph{located}, so a paper whose repository was
unreachable could still enter the sample once an author supplied the address,
whereas an unreachable address ends the attempt here. And their ladder stops at
the build: it does not ask whether the artifact then executes, emits output, or
reproduces a published number, which is where most of our remaining attrition
occurs.

\citet{Pimentel2019} executed 788,781 Jupyter notebooks drawn from a corpus of
1,159,166, and report a two-stage funnel rather than a single rate: 24.11\%
executed without exceptions, and 4.03\% additionally reproduced the results
stored in the notebook. That is the closest precedent for separating \emph{ran}
from \emph{matched}, on a scale far larger than ours, though the artifacts are
notebooks harvested from GitHub rather than the code a published paper points its
readers to, and the two stages are not decomposed into where in the pipeline an
artifact stopped.

Economics has run the closest analog to our experiment, and its result is worth
sitting beside ours. \citet{Gertler2018} took 415 articles published in nine
leading economics journals in a single month, identified 203 empirical papers
without proprietary or restricted data, downloaded what the journals held, and
tried to execute it, allowing up to four hours per paper. Estimation code was the
file most often supplied, and it ran in 40\% of the cases where it was present;
they reproduced the published tables and figures from estimation data for 37\% of
studies, and from raw data for 14\%.

The difference from our setting is the source of the artifact rather than the
discipline. Their files come from a journal's archive, deposited under an
editorial policy by an author who knew an editor would look, and a human
replicator worked on each for up to four hours. Ours come from whatever address
the paper printed, and nothing is repaired by hand. Read together, the two
suggest that most of what an archive supplies is not the code but the conditions
around it.

Computer science has also institutionalized artifact evaluation at systems and
security venues, where committees run submitted artifacts and report substantial
attrition between what is advertised and what builds. That work is a human
judgment made by a reviewer who may correspond with the authors, which makes it
stronger evidence about an individual artifact and weaker evidence about a
distribution. Ordered tiers exist there too, in the form of badge schemes, but
they are awarded by people rather than decided mechanically.

\subsection{What is new here}\label{sec:novelty}

The gap this paper fills is the unassisted, cold-start case in finance: not code
supplied for a coordinated study with data attached, but whatever a published
paper points a reader to, retrieved from that pointer, on a clean machine, with
no data supplied and no author contact. Read against \citet{Perignon2024}, the
question is not whether finance code reproduces under favorable conditions, but
how much of that 52\% survives the removal of the favorable conditions.

What is new is narrower than an ordered scale, and it is worth being exact about
which part, because ordered scales for reproducibility are neither rare nor
recent. \citet{Vandewalle2009} defined six degrees running from ``cannot be
reproduced'' to ``reproduced in fifteen minutes with free tools'' and applied
them to 134 signal-processing papers. \citet{Peng2011} set out a five-point
spectrum from publication alone to full replication. The ACM artifact-badging
policy distinguishes cumulative levels within its Artifacts Evaluated family
\citep{ACMBadging2020}. \citet{Collberg2015} graded build success at three
levels, and \citet{Pimentel2019} separated execution from output agreement.
Closest of all in shape, the ACRE guide's ten-point scale runs from no artifact
available, through an artifact that fails to run, to one that reproduces the
published display item \citep{ACREGuide}, which is the same axis and the same
terminal condition as ours.

We therefore claim no novelty for ordinality, for cumulativeness, or for ending
at a numeric match. Those exist.

What we have not found elsewhere is a scale of this kind that is decided
\emph{mechanically}. Every scheme above is assigned by a person: reviewers in
\citet{Vandewalle2009}, an associate editor in the kite-mark system
\citet{Peng2011} describes, an evaluation committee under
\citep{ACMBadging2020}, a trained reproducer under \citep{ACREGuide}. Their
upper levels turn on judgments such as ``considerable effort'' or ``extreme
effort'', which are informative about an artifact and hard to compare across
scorers. Our rungs 2 to 9 are decided by a program with no human in the loop, so
the same corpus yields the same curve for anyone who runs it \emph{at the same
moment}, and the scale can be applied to hundreds of artifacts rather than to the
dozens a committee can review. The qualification is not rhetorical: the harness
clones a default branch and resolves dependencies against a live package index,
so it removes the scorer from the measurement but not the date, and
\Cref{sec:limitations} treats the resulting timestamp as a limitation rather than
a detail.

Three further properties travel with that. Retrieval, environment declaration,
environment construction and execution are separate rungs rather than a single
build step, so the loss can be located. Each failure carries a reason from a
closed vocabulary tied to the rung that blocked it, so the mix is a table rather
than a narrative. And the whole thing is applied cold, from the address a
published paper gives its readers, with no data supplied and no author contact. That last condition is what separates this from \citet{Collberg2015},
who emailed authors, and from \citet{Perignon2024}, who had the teams and the
data.

The consequence is a curve with a diagnosable shape rather than one number: the
result is not simply that little runs, but that most of the loss occurs at
environment declaration and construction rather than at link rot or at execution
itself. The harness is released as an instrument because the quantity is not
stable: repositories are archived and packages are yanked, and a study that
cannot be re-run cannot tell decay from a difference in cohort.
